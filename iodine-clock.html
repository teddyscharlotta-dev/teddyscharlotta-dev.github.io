Iodine Clock Calibration for a Chem-E-Car Stopping Mechanism

Bottom line Ran 262 timed iodine clock trials over two seasons and reduced them to a concentration–time calibration the team uses to set a stopping distance. Time rises linearly with ascorbic acid concentration from 0.05 to 0.20 M (t = 933 C − 29.0, R² = 0.99 this season), so a target stop time comes off the fit instead of out of a fresh round of testing. Precision is still the weak point. Run-to-run variation fell at 0.125 M, from 9.8% to 4.8% CV, and rose at the other four concentrations.

The problem

A Chem-E-Car gets its target distance about an hour before it runs, and it has to stop there on its own, with no driver and no electronic control of the stopping event. Our team triggers the stop with an iodine clock reaction. Two solutions are combined, the mixture stays clear for a set interval, and the starch–iodine complex appears all at once and cuts the motor. That makes reproducibility the whole problem. A reaction that averages the right time but scatters by fifteen percent will still miss the distance.

What I did

I ran the testing with one other team member. Each trial used two beakers, ascorbic acid and iodine tincture in 30 mL of distilled water in one, hydrogen peroxide and starch indicator in the other. Water and peroxide volumes were fixed, so the mass of vitamin C was the only thing that changed between concentrations. Starch went in as four drops rather than a measured volume. We timed the interval to color change at 0.05, 0.075, 0.10, 0.125, 0.15 and 0.20 M last season, and at the same set minus 0.075 M this season, with fifteen to thirty replicates at each concentration.

Data analysis

Time to color change rises linearly with concentration. Least-squares fits give t = 933 C − 29.0 for this season and t = 771 C − 10.1 for last, both at R² = 0.99, so a stopping time can be set from reagent mass instead of a fresh round of testing. Trials are excluded only where the log records a procedural deviation: eleven that were stirred by hand, and three at 0.05 M flagged for suspected measurement error and possibly a different iodine tincture. Those three alone take the 0.05 M coefficient of variation from 15% to 86%.

Precision got worse at every concentration except 0.125 M, where CV fell from 9.8% to 4.8%. The bench notes point at the solutions rather than the reaction. Several sessions record a new batch of vitamin C or peroxide made partway through, two record the peroxide run down to the last of the bottle, and one notes that a fresh starch batch with much larger drops still gave similar times, which says the four-drop indicator measure is loose. The next round isolates those: one concentration, fresh peroxide against aged, same operator, hand stirring against the plate.

Skills practiced

  • Gravimetric reagent preparation and solution makeup to a target molarity
  • Fixed-protocol replicate testing with a single controlled variable
  • Statistical data reduction: mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation
  • Least-squares regression and calibration curve development
  • Outlier handling tied to a logged procedural cause rather than to fit
  • Excel data management across a multi-season data set
  • Reading run-to-run scatter back to specific bench conditions
  • Designing a follow-up experiment to isolate a suspected variance source
