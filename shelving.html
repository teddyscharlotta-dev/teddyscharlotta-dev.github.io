Angled Racking for a Prepreg Cold-Storage Freezer

Bottom line Designed and welded steel racking that fits 60 in prepreg rolls into 42 in of usable depth by storing them on a 49° angle, recovering the full length of a 90 × 418 in walk-in freezer without narrowing the pallet aisle. Modeled in SketchUp, fabricated by a two-person team, and in daily use.

The problem

Prepreg has to stay below freezing to keep the resin from advancing, so the shop's inventory lives in a walk-in freezer measuring 90 in deep by 418 in long. Pallet carriers have to drive the full length of it, which reserves a 48 in aisle and leaves only 42 in of depth along the wall for storage. The boxes are 60 in long. Laid flat in any orientation, they do not fit in the space that's left, which is why the freezer had been running as open floor stacking with most of its volume unused.

The constraint that set the design

Working from the largest box in inventory, 60 × 14 × 14 in, I budgeted a 64 in slot to cover the tube wall on both sides plus enough clearance to slide a box in and out without fighting it. That set up the governing geometry: a 64 in slot has to fit inside 42 in of depth, so the shelves had to tilt.

The angle falls straight out of that ratio. cos−1(42/64) = 49°, and every other dimension in the design follows from it. Tilting the slot back to 49° puts a 64 in box inside exactly 42 in of horizontal depth, so the aisle stays a full four feet wide and the racking still runs the entire 418 in length of the freezer.

The rest of the requirements

  • Load. Each level carries roughly 100 lb boxes without deflecting enough to bind the box above it.
  • Access. Every box reachable without unstacking, which was the original complaint. Floor stacking meant the weight of a pile sat on the material at the bottom and only the top box was usable.
  • Environment. A freezer opened repeatedly cycles through the dew point, so the structure sees condensation and frost. Weldability and a section that sheds frost mattered more than minimum weight.

What I did

I measured the freezer interior and the inventory, then modeled the racking in SketchUp with the boxes included as solids so the angle, the slot pitch, and the aisle could be checked against each other rather than one at a time. Several configurations went through before the layout settled. The single-bay study above is one of the early ones; the version we built dropped the feet and picked up an additional set of ribs after the load path made more sense with the frame sitting directly on the floor.

For structure I specified 1.5 in square steel tube. It carried the load with margin at these spans, it welds cleanly, and the closed section gives frost far less surface to collect on than an open channel or angle would. I then fabricated the unit with one other team member, welding the frame and shelf levels to the modeled dimensions.

What I learned

The useful lesson was that the binding constraint is rarely the one in the request. I was asked for shelves. What actually decided the design was a four-foot aisle I could not touch and a box that was longer than the space left over, and once those two numbers were on paper the angle was arithmetic rather than a judgment call. Getting to that point took longer than the modeling did.

The second lesson was about fabrication. A few joints that were trivial to draw in SketchUp were awkward to reach with a torch once the frame was tacked together, and the order we welded in ended up mattering as much as the geometry. On the next one I would sketch the weld sequence alongside the model instead of treating fabrication as what happens after the design is done.

Skills practiced

  • Design to a fixed envelope under a non-negotiable access constraint
  • Geometric problem solving and dimensional analysis
  • SketchUp solid modeling, including modeling the payload to verify fit
  • Structural material selection for load and service environment
  • Steel fabrication and welding in a two-person team
