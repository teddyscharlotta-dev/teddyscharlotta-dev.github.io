Iodine Clock Calibration for a Chem-E-Car Stopping Mechanism

Summary Ran 262 timed iodine clock trials over two seasons across six ascorbic acid concentrations. The reaction is what cuts the motor on our Chem-E-Car, so the time it takes to change color sets how far the car travels. Turning the trials into a calibration curve lets the team pick a reagent concentration for whatever distance is announced, instead of testing from scratch on competition day.

The problem

A Chem-E-Car gets its target distance about an hour before it runs, and it has to stop there on its own, with no driver and no electronic control of the stopping event. Our team triggers the stop with an iodine clock reaction. Two solutions are combined, the mixture stays clear for a set interval, and then the starch–iodine complex appears all at once and cuts the motor. So the reaction has to be reproducible. If the timing averages out right but scatters by fifteen percent from run to run, the car still misses the distance.

What I did

I ran the testing with one other team member. Each trial used two beakers: ascorbic acid and iodine tincture in 30 mL of distilled water in one, and hydrogen peroxide and starch indicator in the other. Water and peroxide volumes were fixed, so the mass of vitamin C was the only thing that changed between concentrations. Starch went in as four drops rather than a measured volume. In the 2024–25 season we timed the color change at 0.050, 0.075, 0.100, 0.125, 0.150, and 0.200 M. In 2025–26 we ran the same set without 0.075 M. Each concentration got fifteen to thirty replicates per season, some of them split across two test sets.

Data analysis

Concentration and time to color change fall on a straight line. Least-squares fits through each test set's average give C = 0.001054 t + 0.03323 for 2025–26 (R² = 0.9838) and C = 0.001278 t + 0.01451 for 2024–25 (R² = 0.9805), with C in M and t in seconds. That lets the team read off the concentration for a target stopping time instead of running a new round of tests. The two seasons do not line up, though: at 0.200 M the color change took 156.4 s in 2025–26 and 139.5 s in 2024–25. A curve from one season should be checked against fresh reagents before it is trusted the next.

Flagged trials were left out: eleven that were stirred by hand, and three 2025–26 runs at 0.050 M marked as suspected measurement error, possibly with a different iodine tincture. Including those three would raise the 2025–26 0.050 M coefficient of variation from 15% to 86%. The 2025–26 0.125 M mean uses the first 15 of 26 runs at that concentration.

Run-to-run precision was worse in 2025–26 at every concentration except 0.125 M, where the CV went from 9.8% to 4.1%. Most of the 2024–25 figure comes from two separate 0.125 M sets that averaged 94.2 s and 78.5 s; within each set the CV was 3.5% and 5.5%. The bench notes record several things that could add scatter: new batches of vitamin C or peroxide made partway through a set, peroxide run down to the last of the bottle, a 4 s delay before stirring started, and a ring stand adjusted mid-set for better stirring contact. One note also suggests the starch solution was weaker than planned, since a new batch dispensed in larger drops gave similar times. The next round tests one concentration with fresh vs. aged peroxide, a single operator, and hand stirring vs. the stir plate.
